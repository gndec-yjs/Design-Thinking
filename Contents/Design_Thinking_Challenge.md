As part of the **Design Thinking** course, all students are required to work on a **common real-world problem identified within the college campus**.

### 🧩 The Challenge

A particular stretch of the college corridors has been experiencing **frequent breaking, dislocation and/or misalignment of floor tiles**.

Despite repeated occurrences, the objective of this activity is **not simply to replace the damaged tiles**.

Your task is to understand **why this problem occurs, who is affected, what difficulties it creates, and what a better solution could look like** by following the principles of **Design Thinking**.

> **Do not jump directly to a technical solution.**
>
> **First understand the people, the place and the problem.**

## 👥 Team Formation

* Students shall work in **teams of maximum two members**.
* Each team will investigate the **same campus problem**, but is expected to develop its **own understanding, analysis and solution**.
* Both team members should actively participate in field observation, analysis, ideation and documentation.

## 🔍 Your Design Thinking Journey

During the allotted period, follow the Design Thinking process introduced in the course:

### **1. OBSERVE — Go to the Site**

Visit the affected corridor and carefully observe the actual situation.

Document:

* Condition and location of damaged/displaced tiles
* Pattern and extent of damage
* Surrounding conditions
* User movement and activities
* Any visible contributing factors
* Differences between affected and unaffected areas

Use **photographs, field notes, sketches and basic measurements** wherever relevant.

### **2. LISTEN & UNDERSTAND — Talk to People**

Identify and interact with people who experience or are connected to the problem.

For example:

* Students
* Faculty/staff
* Housekeeping/maintenance workers
* Contractors/workers, where relevant
* Other users of the corridor

Do not assume that you already know their problems.

Ask questions, listen carefully and try to understand their:

**Needs • Experiences • Expectations • Frustrations • Concerns**

### **3. DEFINE — Identify the Real Problem**

The visible problem is:

> **“Floor tiles are frequently breaking, getting displaced or becoming misaligned.”**

But this is only the **starting observation**.

Your team must investigate further and develop a **clear, evidence-based and solution-neutral problem statement**.

Ask:

> **What is actually happening?**
> **Who is affected?**
> **When and where does it happen?**
> **Why might it be happening?**
> **What evidence supports our understanding?**

### **4. ANALYZE — Find the Root Cause**

Do not stop at the visible symptom.

Use appropriate Design Thinking analysis tools discussed in the course, particularly:

* **5-Why Analysis**
* **Fishbone Diagram**
* Stakeholder identification
* Stakeholder mapping
* Identification of user pain points and expectations

Your objective is to move from:

**VISIBLE SYMPTOM → UNDERSTANDING → ROOT CAUSE**

Remember:

> **The first explanation is not necessarily the real cause.**

### **5. IDEATE — Explore Possibilities**

Only after understanding and analysing the problem should you begin developing solutions.

Generate **multiple possible ideas** before selecting one.

Think beyond conventional repair practices.

Consider:

* User needs
* Safety
* Cost
* Constructability
* Maintenance
* Durability
* Time
* Site conditions
* Environmental considerations

The objective is not to find the first solution that comes to mind, but to explore **better possibilities**.

### **6. PROTOTYPE, TEST & IMPROVE**

Develop a simple representation or prototype of your proposed solution wherever feasible.

Then ask:

> **Will it actually work in the real environment?**

Consider how your idea could be tested, what assumptions it depends upon, and how users or other stakeholders might respond.

Use feedback and observations to **improve your concept**.

# 🎯 The Core Principle

### **DO NOT START WITH THE SOLUTION.**

Instead:

**OBSERVE → UNDERSTAND → DEFINE → ANALYZE → IDEATE → PROTOTYPE → TEST → IMPROVE**

The process is **iterative**. Testing may take you back to an earlier stage when new evidence changes your understanding.

## 📋 What Your Team Should Produce

By the end of the activity, your team should be able to demonstrate:

* Evidence from **field observation**
* Identification of relevant **stakeholders**
* User experiences, needs and pain points
* Photographs, sketches and basic measurements where applicable
* A clearly defined **problem statement**
* **5-Why / root-cause analysis**
* Identification of important constraints
* A **How Might We (HMW)** question
* Multiple solution ideas
* A selected concept with justification
* Prototype/testing approach, wherever feasible
* Key learning and proposed improvements

## ⚠️ Important

This is **not a conventional engineering design exercise** in which you are expected to immediately identify the technical cause and prescribe a repair.

Your evaluation will focus on **how you approached the problem**:

> **Did you observe before assuming?**
> **Did you listen to users?**
> **Did you investigate the root cause?**
> **Did you consider different perspectives?**
> **Did you explore multiple possibilities?**
> **Did you test and learn from evidence?**

### **The goal is not simply to fix a broken tile.**

### **The goal is to understand why the problem exists and design a better way forward.**
