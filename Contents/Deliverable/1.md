## 1. Learning Objectives

After completing this unit, students should be able to:

* Explain the concept and importance of **Design Thinking** in engineering.
* Differentiate between **conventional engineering design** and Design Thinking.
* Describe the major stages of the Design Thinking process.
* Observe civil engineering problems from the perspective of users and stakeholders.
* Identify real-world problems through systematic field observation.
* Record observations using photographs, sketches, notes, and basic measurements.
* Convert field observations into clearly defined problem opportunities.

## 2. What is Design Thinking?

**Design Thinking** is a human-centred, problem-solving approach that focuses on understanding people, identifying their actual needs, generating multiple possible solutions, developing prototypes, and improving those solutions through testing and feedback.

In civil engineering, Design Thinking can be used to understand problems that are not purely technical but involve **people, infrastructure, environment, cost, safety, accessibility, usability and local conditions**.

For example, consider waterlogging near a college entrance.

A conventional approach may begin with:

> "Increase the size of the drain."

A Design Thinking approach first asks:

* Where exactly does water accumulate?
* When does waterlogging occur?
* Who is affected?
* How do pedestrians, students and vehicles move through the area?
* Is the problem due to inadequate drainage capacity, blocked inlets, incorrect slope, surface runoff, or some combination?
* What do users experience during rainfall?
* What solutions are feasible considering cost, maintenance and site constraints?

The objective is therefore not simply to **design a technically correct solution**, but to understand the **real problem before designing the solution**.

## 3. Conventional Design vs Design Thinking

Traditional engineering design generally starts with a defined technical problem and proceeds towards analysis and design.

Design Thinking places greater emphasis on **understanding the problem and the people affected by it before selecting a solution**.

| Conventional Engineering Design                      | Design Thinking                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Problem is generally predefined                      | Problem is explored and defined through observation             |
| Primarily solution-oriented                          | Problem- and user-oriented                                      |
| Often begins with technical requirements             | Begins by understanding users and context                       |
| May focus mainly on technical performance            | Considers technical, social, economic and environmental aspects |
| Usually follows a relatively linear process          | Iterative process with repeated improvement                     |
| One or limited alternatives may be developed         | Multiple alternatives are encouraged                            |
| Testing often occurs after design development        | Testing and feedback are integrated into the process            |
| Success is often measured through technical criteria | Success considers technical performance and user needs          |

### Civil Engineering Example

**Problem:** Pedestrians are facing difficulty near a campus gate during peak hours.

A conventional response might be:

> "Widen the entrance road."

A Design Thinking investigation may reveal that the real problem is caused by:

* Poor pedestrian-vehicle segregation
* Inadequate footpath width
* Improper parking near the entrance
* Lack of pedestrian crossing
* Poor visibility
* Confusing circulation pattern

The final solution may therefore involve **pedestrian segregation, improved circulation, markings, signage and controlled parking**, rather than simply widening the road.

## 4. The Design Thinking Process

Design Thinking is an **iterative process**. The stages may be represented as:

**Observe → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Improve ↺**

The process does not always move in a straight line. Testing may reveal that the original problem was misunderstood, requiring the team to return to the **Observe** or **Define** stage.

## 4.1 Observe

The first stage is to **understand the situation through direct observation**.

Students should go to the actual site, observe users and infrastructure, and collect evidence rather than relying only on assumptions.

### Civil Engineering Examples

Students may observe:

* Water accumulation on a campus road after rainfall  
* Pedestrians walking on the carriageway because of an inadequate footpath  
* Improper waste segregation around a hostel  
* Congestion near a parking area  
* Cracks or deterioration in a pavement  
* Unsafe movement of workers at a construction site  
* Poor accessibility for elderly or differently-abled users  
* Dust generation around construction activities  
* Dampness, seepage or water leakage in building walls  
* Damp patches and deterioration of plaster or finishes  
* Corrosion-related cracking or spalling of reinforced concrete members  
* Exposed or deteriorated reinforcement in an RCC structure  
* Poor thermal comfort or excessive heat gain in a building  
* Inadequate natural lighting or ventilation in occupied spaces  
* Water wastage due to leaking fixtures or inefficient plumbing arrangements  
* Poor rainwater harvesting or stormwater management in a building or campus  
* Inefficient use of conventional construction materials  
* Excessive generation of construction and demolition (C&D) waste  
* Poor segregation or reuse of construction waste at a construction site  
* Difficulty in using recycled aggregates or recycled materials in construction  
* High material consumption or wastage during construction  
* Excessive dependence on cement-intensive construction practices  
* High-energy or resource-intensive construction methods  
* Poor durability of building materials under local environmental exposure  
* Maintenance problems caused by unsuitable material selection  
* Difficulty in maintaining conventional building components  
* Lack of affordable and sustainable construction alternatives  
* Challenges in adopting low-carbon or recycled construction materials  
* Problems associated with transportation and storage of construction materials  
* Safety and usability problems associated with temporary construction arrangements  
* Infrastructure that performs technically but does not adequately meet user needs  

These examples demonstrate that a Design Thinking problem does not have to be a major structural failure. A relatively small problem affecting **usability, durability, maintenance, safety, sustainability, cost or user experience** can also become a meaningful engineering design challenge.

### Key Question

> **What is actually happening at the site?**

## 4.2 Define

The observations are analysed to identify the **actual problem**.

A good problem statement should describe:

* Who is experiencing the problem?
* What difficulty are they facing?
* Where does it occur?
* Under what conditions?
* What are the major constraints?

### Example

**Weak problem statement:**

> "The campus drainage system needs improvement."

**Better problem statement:**

> "During intense rainfall, pedestrians using the main campus entrance experience difficulty crossing the access road because surface runoff accumulates near the pedestrian movement area."

The second statement describes an **observed situation and user problem** without prematurely prescribing a solution.

## 4.3 Ideate

In this stage, students generate **multiple possible solutions**.

The purpose is not to immediately select the first idea that appears practical.

For a campus drainage problem, possible ideas might include:

* Additional surface inlets
* Regrading of the pavement
* Improvement of drain connectivity
* Permeable pavement
* Rain garden
* Recharge pit
* Modified kerb arrangement
* Combination of drainage and infiltration measures

The ideas can subsequently be screened based on **technical feasibility, cost, safety, sustainability, constructability and maintenance**.

## 4.4 Prototype

A prototype is a **simplified representation of a proposed solution** that allows the team to explore and communicate the idea.

A civil engineering prototype may be:

* Hand sketch
* Site layout
* Drainage plan
* Physical model
* Scaled model
* Mock-up
* Flow diagram
* 3D model
* AutoCAD drawing
* SketchUp model
* Digital visualization

### Example

For a proposed pedestrian safety improvement, a prototype could be a **scaled campus entrance model showing the proposed footpath, pedestrian crossing, bollards and vehicle circulation**.

A prototype does not need to be a final construction-ready design.

> **Prototype to learn, not simply to present.**

## 4.5 Test

The proposed solution is tested with users, peers or technical experts.

Testing may involve:

* User feedback
* Peer review
* Expert review
* Demonstration
* Physical testing
* Model-based testing
* Comparison with existing conditions

For example, a proposed pedestrian circulation arrangement can be shown to students and security staff to determine whether:

* The movement path is clear.
* Pedestrian-vehicle conflicts are reduced.
* The arrangement is easy to understand.
* Emergency access is maintained.

## 4.6 Improve

The feedback obtained during testing is used to **modify and improve the solution**.

For example:

**Prototype 1 → User feedback → Modification → Prototype 2 → Testing → Improved design**

This iterative approach is particularly important in civil engineering because a technically feasible solution may still face practical problems related to:

* Construction
* Cost
* Maintenance
* User behaviour
* Accessibility
* Safety
* Site constraints

## 6. Field Observation and Problem Identification

Field observation is the starting point of the practical component of this course.

Students should avoid selecting a problem only because it appears interesting. The problem should preferably be:

* **Observable**
* **Relevant to users**
* **Specific**
* **Manageable within the course duration**
* **Possible to investigate with available resources**
* **Suitable for developing and testing a prototype**

## 6.1 Observation Walk

An **observation walk** involves systematically moving through a selected site and recording problems, user behaviour and environmental conditions.

### During an observation walk, look for:

**People**

* Who uses the space?
* How do they move?
* Where do they stop?
* What difficulties do they face?

**Infrastructure**

* What is damaged, inadequate or poorly arranged?
* Are facilities accessible and usable?

**Movement**

* Where do conflicts occur between pedestrians and vehicles?
* Are circulation paths clear?

**Environment**

* Is there waterlogging, dust, noise, waste or poor ventilation?
* How does weather affect the problem?

**Safety**

* Are there unsafe locations or activities?
* Are warning signs, barriers or protective measures adequate?

## 6.2 Site Survey

A basic site survey helps convert observations into measurable evidence.

Students may record:

* Approximate dimensions
* Widths and lengths
* Slopes or levels where relevant
* Locations of drains, entrances, obstacles, etc.
* Number of users or vehicles during a selected period
* Time and duration of occurrence
* Environmental conditions

### Example

Instead of writing:

> "The road is congested."

Record observations such as:

> "During the 1:00–1:15 PM observation period, vehicles and pedestrians were observed competing for movement space near the main entrance."

The second observation is more useful because it can be further investigated and validated.

## 7. Photo Documentation

Photographs provide visual evidence of the existing condition.

Students should:

* Capture photographs from useful viewpoints.
* Avoid taking random photographs without purpose.
* Record the location and date where appropriate.
* Use photographs to support observations.
* Mark or annotate important features when necessary.
* Maintain privacy and avoid unnecessarily identifying individuals.

### Example

For a drainage problem, photographs may show:

1. Existing road condition
2. Location of water accumulation
3. Drain inlet
4. Direction of surface runoff
5. Pedestrian movement affected by waterlogging

Each photograph should have a meaningful caption.

**Example:**

> *Figure 1: Water accumulation observed near the pedestrian movement area at the campus entrance after rainfall.*

## 8. Sketches and Basic Measurements

A simple sketch can often communicate a problem more effectively than a photograph alone.

Students may prepare:

* Site sketches
* Plan views
* Section sketches
* Flow diagrams
* Movement diagrams
* Annotated photographs

The sketch should indicate important features such as:

* Direction of movement
* Approximate dimensions
* Location of defects/problems
* Drainage direction
* Entry and exit points
* Major obstacles

The objective is **not artistic quality**. The objective is clear communication of the observed problem.

## 9. From Observation to Problem

Students should distinguish between an **observation**, a **symptom**, and a **problem**.

### Example: Campus Drainage

**Observation:**
Water was observed accumulated near the campus gate after rainfall.

**Symptom:**
Pedestrians are forced to move around the accumulated water.

**Possible underlying problem:**
Surface runoff is not being effectively collected or conveyed away from the pedestrian movement area.

Further investigation is required before deciding the actual root cause.

> **Do not assume the solution before understanding the problem.**

For example, "construct a bigger drain" is a **solution**, not a problem statement.

## 10. Good Problem Identification

A good Design Thinking problem should satisfy the following:

### Specific

Avoid very broad problems such as:

> "Improve the entire campus."

Instead:

> "Improve pedestrian movement near the main campus gate during peak hours."

### User-Centred

Identify who is affected:

> Students, visitors, security personnel, staff, pedestrians, workers, etc.

### Evidence-Based

Support the problem with:

* Photographs
* Field notes
* Measurements
* Sketches
* Observations
* User interactions

### Solution-Neutral

Do not define the solution too early.

**Avoid:**

> "Design a new drain."

**Prefer:**

> "Reduce recurring water accumulation near the campus entrance that disrupts pedestrian movement during rainfall."

## 11. Field Observation Checklist

Before leaving the site, make sure you have recorded:

* [ ] Location of the problem
* [ ] Date and time of observation
* [ ] Users/stakeholders affected
* [ ] Photographs
* [ ] Field notes
* [ ] Relevant measurements
* [ ] Sketches or annotated photographs
* [ ] Existing infrastructure/conditions
* [ ] Frequency or circumstances of the problem
* [ ] Initial assumptions/questions requiring further investigation

## 14. Key Takeaway

> **Design Thinking starts with understanding the problem, not with designing the solution.**

As civil engineers, learn to **observe the built environment, understand the people who use it, identify what is actually going wrong, and collect evidence before proposing a solution.**
